    What functions do you know “by name”? We’ll see more of these later, but for the moment, ask yourself if you can pair a formula or a graph with each of those names?
    🔗
    You have likely used a variety of formulas and functions throughout math and science classes, research, or work. In math, we typically say something like
    \begin{equation*} y = f(x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Keep in mind that you use lots of formulas elsewhere with a variety of different letters. We will usually refer to functions as \(f(x)\text{,}\) but in applications might try to use letters like \(g = g(w)\) where we use the same letter for the output variable and function name - this might seem weird, but it’s actually less to worry about and gives more clues about the relationship and how to use it. Before talking more about specific examples and applications of functions, let’s just give an example of relationships between quantities that might or might not illustrate functions and try to recognize what goes wrong.
    🔗
    The most important example of a function to start is the linear function. We use these as for a variety of reasons in mathematics and science. You may recall,
    \begin{equation*} y = f(x) = mx+b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It turns out, this isn’t as useful as you may have been lead to believe. A more versatile formula is,
    \begin{equation*} y = f(x) = y_0 + m(x - x_0). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Fortunately, these are largely interchangeable, it just depends on what information we have access to as we get started.
    🔗
    To start to see how these are connected, what’s something that both formulas have in common?
    🔗
    You might be thinking about scatterplots and “trend lines”, which is a good place to be. That process actually uses some of the calculus we will encounter later. To get started we will be a bit more casual.

    \begin{equation*} y = f(x) = mx+b, \end{equation*}

    \begin{equation*} y = f(x) = y_0 + m(x - x_0). \end{equation*}
    Much of calculus is about the slope of the graph of a function at a point, so that is precisely the information we have to work with. Additionally, intuition we have built using linear functions in algebra and in science applications should prove useful in calculus.

    🔗
    🔗

    Final thoughts.

    Linear functions are very important throughout mathematics and statistics, the sciences, and beyond. A few important facts.
    • They can be simplified to the form
      \begin{equation*} (\text{output}) = \text{constant})\times\text{input}) + \text{constant}). \end{equation*}
      Usually those constants, or parameters are called the slope and y-intercept and represented by the symbols \(m\) and \(b\text{,}\) respectively.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Perhaps less well-known, linear functions written using point-slope, where \(m\)remains the slope and \(x_{0}\) and \(x_{0}\) are coordinates in the point \(x_{0}\text{,}\) are useful for calculus purposes and extend well to later extensions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Depending on the context, you may see this written in as any of the following, where, within each, hopefully the meaning of all parameters is intuitive,
      • \(y = mx+b\text{,}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • \(y = a + bx\text{,}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • \(y = a_{0} + a_{1}x\text{,}\) or
        🔗
        🔗
      • \(y = y_{0} + m(x-x_{0})\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      What is important is the structure of the formula, not the specific way that it is written.
      🔗
      🔗
    • On standard axes, the graph will always be a straight line.
      🔗
      🔗
