    Section 2.1 Rates of change

    The slope of a linear function, often interpreted as rise over run, describes the ratio of the change in output over the change in input for any two points on the graph. Since graphs of linear functions are straight lines, and their slope never changes, it does not matter which points are selected. Depending on the meaning of the function (i.e., the relationship beween its variables), the slope can be interpreted as the rate of change of the output with respect to the input.
    🔗
    Linear functions are important throughout math and science, and become useful reasoning tools for better understanding more complicated nonlinear relationships between two variables. We can understand the average rate of change of a function over some interval, by finding the slope of the line that connects those two points on the graph of the function, called a secant line. This association provides mechanism to describe the average rate of change of that function, over the horizontal interval between the two points.
    🔗
    If you plotted the distance you traveled as a function of time as you drove to campus or as you walked between classes, between any two distinct points on that graph you could calculate your average velocity over that interval. The average velocity would describe the change in distance traveled divided by the interval of time over which you traveled. If you measured distance in meters and time in seconds, your average velocity would be in meters per second. If you measured distance in miles and time in hours, your average velocity would be in miles per hour. Of course if you measured either time in minutes or distance in kilometers, you might have to do a unit conversion to get a more sensible unit to help with interpretation.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.1 Visualizing rates of change

    In Figure 2.1.1 through Figure 2.1.4, you will be guided through a sequence of very similar, but increasingly abstract representations of these ideas. The goal is not to make this abstract, but to extend calculations we are used to performing arithmetically (i.e., with our calculators) to things more useful for calculus that must be done algebraically (i.e., with pencil and paper). Proceeding from a very concrete example in Figure 2.1.1, we will slowly introduce the necessary generalizations in framing this problem to make it more useful in the context of calculus.
    🔗
    In Figure 2.1.1, we knew exactly where our second point on the graph was placed, specifically at the point \((3, f(3)) = (3, 9)\text{.}\) Aside from using functions to generate the output values, this should feel familiar to how we have used two points (four numbers) to find the slopes of linear functions so far. Now we will transition towards making this problem and calculation a bit more general, and in doing so trade a few numbers for symbols. In Figure 2.1.2–2.1.4, the graph and secant lines are actually the same exact mathematical functions, nothing about the content changes. What does change, very strategically I hope, is the emphasis on what information is labeled, in an attempt to highlight what is essential to the new concept. Once again, we are only thinking about "rise over run" or "change in output over change in input".
    🔗
    We can generalize this a bit by asking "what if our second point was some distance (to the right) of our first point?". In doing so, we shift or focus from where the second point is located to how far the second point is from the first point. We let \(h\) represent the horizontal distance from the first point to the second point. Instead of choosing the locations of the two points, we choose the first point and then specify the "run".
    🔗
    Suppressing the detailed axes tick marks, or focus is on the locations of the two points and the slope of the line that connects them. Despite introducing the symbol \(h\) into the coordinate of the second point, our goal remains to calculate the slope of the line that connects the point \((1, 1)\) to the second point \((1+h, f(1+h))\text{.}\) In general,
    \begin{align*} m_{\text{sec}} & = \frac{\text{change in output}}{\text{change in input}}\\ & = \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\\ & = \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{(1+h)-1}\\ m_{\text{sec}} & = \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} \end{align*}
    In the denominator, \(h\) is the horizontal distance between the two points. Since the denominator is of the form \((1+h)) - 1\text{,}\) the "run" will simplify to \(h\) the distance between the points.
    🔗
    Since the function is \(f(x) = x^{2}\text{,}\) we can explicitly calculate the slope as
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{(1+h)^{2} - (1)^{2}}{h} = \frac{(1)^2 + 2(1)(h) + h^2 - (1)^2}{h} = 2 + h\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since the value of \(h\) controls how far away the second point is from the first, in general \(h\) affects the function value at that point. So, the choice of \(h\) also affects the value of the slope of the secant line connecting those points.
    🔗
    Pausing to reflect on Figure 2.1.1, the second point was at \((3, 9)\) which corresponds to \(h = 2\text{.}\) Substituting \(h = 2\) into the new slope formula, the slope of the secant line that connects the point at \((1, 1)\) to a second point \(h = 2\) units to the right, is
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{sec}} = 2 + 2 = 4\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which matches our earlier result.
    🔗
    Going a bit further, there was nothing really special about the choice of \((1, 1)\) as our reference point (aside from the fact that it made the calculations "nice"). Now suppose, as shown in Figure 2.1.3, our first point was at \(x = a\text{,}\) for some number \(a\text{,}\) and that the coordinates of the point were now \((a, f(a))\) and the second point was at \((a + h, f(a+h))\text{.}\) We can still calculate a slope of the secant line connecting these points, but our answer will be a formula in terms of the \(a\)-value that we start the line at and the \(h\)-value that determines how far away our second point is located. Repeating the slope calculations above, we find that
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{sec}} = 2a + h\text{.} \end{equation*}
    now both \(a\) and \(h\) appear in the slope formula, but again comparing to our earlier problem, showin in Figure 2.1.1, where \(a = 1\) and \(h = 2\text{,}\) our "new" slope formula suggests that
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{sec}} = 2(1) + 2 = 4\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally, there was nothing special about using a point marked \((a, f(a))\text{,}\) so the most useful, but perhaps less concrete, way to think about this, is to use some point at \((x, f(x))\) and a second point \((x+h, f(x+h))\text{,}\) \(h\) units away in the horizontal direction. Finally, this gives a forumla for the slope of the secant line connecting these points that is given by,
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{sec}} = 2x + h\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Using this to measure the slope between a point at \(x = 1\) and a second point \(h = 2\) units away in the horizontal direction, we once again find
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{sec}} = 2(1) + 2 = 4\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now, it might feel like we just found increasingly confusing ways to say the same thing, but the idea is that by making slight changes to how we are framing this problem, we are now ready to leap into developing our first real important calculus idea, the derivative. We still have a little bit of work to do though.
    🔗
    Though we have only discussed averages rates of change over intervals so far, after Subsection 2.1.2, we will begin to calculate the instantaneous rate of change of a function at a point.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 Tabulating rates of change

    Consider relationship showin in Figure 2.1.2 between the function\(f(x) = x^{2}\) and its tangent line \(f_{\text{sec}}(x) = f(1) + m_{\text{sec}}(x - 1)\text{,}\) where \((1, f(1))\) is the base point for the secant line. One way to approximate an instantaneous rate of change is to calculate average rates of change as \(h\text{,}\) the horizontal distance between the points, shrinks smaller and smaller, closer to zero. We can organize this "experiment" in a table of useful information, where again, the whole goal is to find a slope.
    🔗
    The evidence in Table 2.1.5 suggests that as \(h\) shrinks to zero and the second point gets closer to the first, the slope of the secant seems to approach a number like \(2\text{.}\) Including one extra row to the Table 2.1.5 as shown in Table 2.1.6, confirms that as \(h\) gets even closer to zero, that the slope of the corresponding secant line gets closer to \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Admittedly the table is clumsy and the patterns are not always clear. A better approach is to take the perspective illustrated in Figure 2.1.4 and, with a bit more elegance, take an algebraic approach. To do this we need the limit definition of the derivative.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.7. limit definition of the derivative.

    For a function \(f(x)\text{,}\) the derivative is given by
    \begin{equation*} f'(x) = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \end{equation*}
    for all \(x\)-values at which the limit exists.
    🔗
    🔗
    Remember that the expression "inside the limit" describes the slope of the secant line that connects the points at \((x, f(x))\) and \((x+h, f(x+h))\) on the graph of the function. So, as suggested in Table 2.1.5–2.1.6, the slope of the tangent line, or derivative, is given by the "limit" of the slope of the secant line as the distance \(h\) between the points shrinks to zero. Conceptually,
    \begin{equation*} m_{\text{tan}} = \lim\limits_{h\to0}m_{\text{sec}}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    with which, we find the slope of the secant line between the points indicated, \((x, f(x))\) and \((x+h, f(x+h))\text{,}\) simplify as much as possible, then substitute the hypothetical distance "between" points of \(h=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Though limits have been underrepresented so far, we will attempt to apply the definition. What it means is "what happens to the value of the expression inside, as \(h\) goes to zero?" Looking at Definition 2.1.7, if we tried to set \(h\) to zero immediately, our idea of slope falls apart, the numerator is
    \begin{equation*} f(x + 0) - f(x)\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which is zero, regardless of what the function is, and somewhat worse, the denominator is zero, resulting in a calculation of the form "zero over zero", which is mathematically undefined. So what we do instead is a bit of algebra "inside the limit" to try to simplify in such a way that we no longer divide by zero. We have done this above, a bit informally, but will repeat it now for this example.
    • We will evaluate the function at \(x+h\) and at \(x\) in the numerator and then simplify that result.
      🔗
      🔗
    • After that, we will combine like terms, which results in all terms from the original function vanishing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • After that, in an act of caution, we factor out an \(h\) from the numerator, which we hope to divide out by the \(h\) from the denominator, so that we can avoid dividing by zero.
      🔗
      🔗
    • At that point, there is an expression (called the "difference quotient") which has been simplified to the point where we can revisit our question "what happens to the value of the expression inside, as \(h\) goes to zero?" What happens? We have the derivative of \(f(x) = x^{2}\) once we substitute zero for \(h\) in the second to last line.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) & = \lim\limits_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\\ & = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2 - (x)^2}{h}\\ & = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\frac{x^2+2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h}\\ & = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\frac{2xh + h^2}{h}\\ & = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\frac{h(2x + h)}{h}\\ & = \lim\limits_{h\to0}2x + h\\ f'(x) & = 2x + 0 = 2x \end{align*}
    Now, by definition we have just shown that the derivative of \(f(x) = x^{2}\) is given by \(f'(x) = 2x\text{.}\) Going forward we will spend a lot more time on the interpretation and application of derivatives, with respect to features of the original function. But for now, the derivative of a function is the slope of the tangent line to the function at the point where the derivative is evaluated — in other words, the derivative gives us a formula we can use to calculate how steep a function is at any point
     1 
    Technicality: this is true at any point where the derivative is defined. The good news is that in most life sciences applications, the functions have nice enough mathematical properties where this is not a concern.
    along its graph. In this case, values of the linear function \(f'(x) = 2x\) give us an exact way to measure how steep the graph of \(f(x) = x^{2}\) is at each point.
    🔗
    Going forward we will see that it is often useful to know where a function is the steepest, or where it is exactly flat.
