    We are building an idea called discrete-time dynamical systems. A dynamical system describes how something changes, and calling it discrete-time means we are talking about changes occurring step-by-step - like daily, weekly, or yearly measurements. The graphical technique is called “cobwebbing” and it’s name becomes clearer as we move ahead.
    🔗
    These equations are examples of mathematical models, and now we can try to build one from a short story. Suppose researchers monitor the dosing of a simple medicine. Omitting units, researchers collect data that suggests that patients absorb half of the dose of medicine present the previous day and are given a new dose to raise the concentration by one unit. If we let \(M_t\) denote the concentration in the bloodstream on day \(t\text{,}\) we can write a mathematical model to predict the dose under this scheme on the following day or days.

    Starting from \(M_0 = 1\) or \(M_0 = 5\text{,}\) two different initial medicine concentrations, calculate the next 3 terms, that is \(M_1, M_2, \text{ and } M_3\text{.}\) To get you started \(M_{n+1} = f(M_n) = 0.5M_{n} + 1\text{,}\) from the previous page, so \(M_1 = 0.5M_0+1\text{,}\) where \(M_0\) is either \(1\) or \(5\text{,}\) your choice.

    This leads to an important concept called an equilibrium point. This is defined as the solution to or equation when input and output are both equal (i.e., our equation doesn’t change anything). We use
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{M_{t+1}}_\text{new (output)} = \underbrace{M_{t}}_\text{old (input)} = M^*, \end{equation*}
    where the asterisk on \(M^*\) just means that it is an important number. If so,
    \begin{equation*} M_{t+1} = f(M_{t}) \text{ becomes } M^* = f(M^*), \end{equation*}
    and we have to solve whatever equation is left for the unknown quantity \(M^*\text{.}\) This is no different from how we would usually solve for an unknown quantity more familiarly called \(x\) in any other equation.
